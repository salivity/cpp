How std::midpoint Prevents Overflow in C++

Calculating the midpoint between two numbers is a fundamental operation in programming, yet traditional implementations frequently introduce subtle, critical bugs. This article explains how the std::midpoint function, introduced in C++20, solves the long-standing problem of arithmetic overflow during integer calculations. By examining standard naive approaches and comparing them with standard library mechanics, this guide details what std::midpoint prevents, how it guarantees safety, and its precise behavior with edge cases.

The Hidden Bug in Naive Midpoint Calculations

The most intuitive way to compute the midpoint of two values, a and b, is:

int mid = (a + b) / 2;

While mathematically sound, this formulation is dangerous in finite-precision computer arithmetic. When a and b are large positive integers, their sum a + b can exceed the maximum representable value of the integer type. In C++, signed integer overflow results in undefined behavior. In practice, this often causes values to wrap around to negative numbers, producing wildly incorrect results. For unsigned integers, the sum wraps modulo \(2^N\), silently returning an invalid midpoint without triggering a compiler warning.

A common attempt to fix this issue is rewriting the expression as:

int mid = a + (b - a) / 2;

While this avoids overflow when a and b share the same sign and b >= a, it fails when a and b have opposite signs. If a is a large negative number and b is a large positive number, the subtraction b - a can exceed the maximum limit of the integer type, triggering the exact overflow issue it intended to prevent.

What std::midpoint Prevents

Found in the <numeric> header since C++20, std::midpoint explicitly prevents arithmetic overflow and undefined behavior across all valid inputs of an integer type.

Specifically, std::midpoint(a, b) guarantees:

  1. No Intermediate Overflow: The function computes the midpoint without ever producing an intermediate value that exceeds the bounds of the type. You do not need to manually cast values to a wider integer type (such as promoting int to int64_t).
  2. Correctness Across the Entire Range: It safely handles edge cases, such as finding the midpoint between std::numeric_limits<int>::min() and std::numeric_limits<int>::max(), where manual formulas typically break down.
  3. Prevention of Sign-Change Inversion: Unlike naive subtraction methods, std::midpoint handles mixed-sign values without overflowing during difference calculations.

Predictable Rounding Behavior

Beyond overflow prevention, std::midpoint provides well-defined rounding semantics. When the exact mathematical midpoint falls between two integers (i.e., when the sum \(a + b\) is odd), std::midpoint is specified to round towards a.

For example:

  • std::midpoint(3, 4) evaluates to 3
  • std::midpoint(4, 3) evaluates to 4
  • std::midpoint(-3, -4) evaluates to -3
  • std::midpoint(-4, -3) evaluates to -4

This deterministic behavior eliminates inconsistencies across different compiler optimizations and architectures. By abstracting the complex bitwise and branching logic required to calculate safe averages, std::midpoint offers a safe, performant, and standard-compliant solution for integer arithmetic.
